What a fresh tyre buys you, right now

The question a race engineer asks with the gap on the screen: I pit now, they respond a lap or two later — does one lap of clear air cover the gap? Both cars serve the same pit lane, so the ~22 s pit loss cancels out of the comparison. That is why undercuts work.

The pit loss cancels — both cars serve the same pit lane, so it drops out of the subtraction. What is left is pace.

This tool does not tell you that you will gain a place. We backtested that claim against 137 real head-to-head duels and it failed: our margin ranks the winner at AUC 0.836, and so does the gap alone with no model. The rate is ours and it is validated. The gap is on every timing screen in the pit lane. So we show the rate, we show the subtraction, and you decide.

The obvious fit says tyres get faster as they wear. It’s fuel.

Fit lap time against tyre age on practice long runs — the standard approach — and two of the three compounds come back claiming a worn tyre is quicker than a fresh one. Inside a run the car burns about a kilogram of fuel for every lap the tyre ages, so the two are collinear and the age slope absorbs the fuel effect, which pulls the other way. We estimated fuel sensitivity on race laps instead, where the same tyre age appears at many fuel loads, and imported it.

Measured against predicted, out of sample

Every point is one real pit stop, scored by a model that never saw its event. Ground truth is the step in pace across the stop: tyre age resets, fuel does not, so the step measures the tyre effect with fuel cancelled by construction. That is why we score against this and not against our own fit.

Diagonal is perfect prediction. Calibration slope is the fitted slope of this cloud — 1.0 would mean the magnitudes are exactly right.

Residuals, predicted minus measured. Centred means unbiased on average; the spread is the RMSE.
